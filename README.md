# Last Tango in Moscow sample
# Chapter 1: The Spy Who Slept
<b><i>Moscow, Russia, June 1988</i></b>
On a bright summer morning, as I entered the silent halls of Moscow State University, I felt like a dead man walking.

If I blew my first assignment and was captured, they’d probably rough me up and deport me. Or they might torture me to death, trying to extract information I didn’t have.

I tried to think happier thoughts, but it’s hard when the name in your ID comes from someone who never lived past his first day.

The poor guy had died at birth twenty-six years ago, but two decades later a faceless colleague of mine had applied for a copy of the man’s birth certificate, then obtained his passport—a genuine Soviet passport with a red cover and a golden state emblem.

This valuable document now resided in the inside pocket of my suit, alongside my bogus high school diploma and other phony documents.

The absurdity of an American agent applying to study English at MSU wasn’t lost on me, and I might have grinned like a lunatic if I hadn’t been scared stiff.

I turned a corner in the long, echoing corridor and approached the reception desk.

A stern middle-aged woman behind the counter was reading a book with a crimson jacket. Probably a KGB manual on detecting foreign agents. But as I came closer, I saw the silhouette of a couple holding hands on the cover.

A love story. I let out a slow breath.

To the right of her counter loomed an open doorway to a large hall filled with widely spaced desks. Behind each desk stood a single chair. Several of them were already occupied.

Good. I didn’t want to draw attention to myself by being the first to arrive.

The large hall was clearly set up for an exam. But the building was hosting multiple entrance tests, and no sign indicated which one would be held here.

I cleared my throat to attract the woman’s attention.

Reluctantly, she lifted her gaze from the book and peered at me over her horn-rimmed glasses, her eyebrows rising in question.

“Good morning,” I said.

She regarded me with curiosity and a touch of motherly amusement. “You’re a bit early.”

“I didn’t want to be late.”

“Admirable.”

I nodded toward the open doorway. “Is this the English exam?”

“Can’t you read, young man?” she asked with typical Russian directness, then turned to look at the empty corkboard behind her. “Oh,” she exclaimed in embarrassment. “Oh my!”

I spotted a square sheet of paper lying face down a few feet away by a potted ficus. I picked it up and turned it over. ENGLISH LANG EXAM was scribbled on it in uneven handwritten letters.

“May I?” I asked, indicating the corkboard.

“If it’s not too much trouble.”

I pinned the sign with pushpins in each corner and stepped back.

She held a pen ready over the open sign-in ledger. “What’s your name?”

“Arthur Veish.”

Scanning the page, she quickly found me near the top. In Cyrillic, V is the third letter. She put a check mark after my name. “Veish. Is that German for white?” she asked, looking up at me.

“No, it’s Latvian for wind.”

“Ah, wind. How curious,” she said wistfully. “Free and fleet and elusive as wind.”

I allowed myself a little laugh. “You can say that again.”

“So, you’re from Latvia?” she asked.

“Yes,” I lied. My ancestors came to America from England, France, and Sweden.

“I don’t believe we have any applicants from Latvia this year.”

“A pity,” I said, hoping my relief didn’t show on my face. Although I had a plausible explanation for why I spoke both Russian and Latvian with an accent, I hoped to avoid putting that excuse to the test.

“And you came all the way here to study English?”

“Yes,” I lied without missing a beat. “MSU is the best university in the Soviet Union.”

“Oh yes, it is,” she said proudly. “Well, good luck to you, Comrade Wind. Go inside, pick up your exam ticket, and take a seat at any desk you like.”

“Thank you.”

I stepped through the doorway.

# Chapter 2 Lucky Thirteen
The hall in which I found myself was vast, even grander than it had appeared through the doorway. Columns soared up to the high ceiling. At least three hundred desks filled the space. The din of the streets was muted by the thick walls, and the air smelled of books and floor wax.

I wondered what devil had inspired the Stalinist architects to such excesses of gigantism.

A wide table stood to the left, by the entrance. On its gleaming mahogany surface lay dozens of large manila envelopes. Exam tickets. Under the Soviet system, the entrance test came in several variants—some harder than others.

Not that any English assignment would be hard for me. My problem would be acing it. I’d add a few errors to be on the safe side.

But if I overcompensated and failed the exam? What would the bosses back at the Company make of that? I didn’t want them thinking they had employed an imbecile.

Half a dozen proctors milled around the entrance, and a couple more sat at the head table with the exam tickets.

A bald middle-aged man with a red, bulbous nose, dressed in a wrinkled navy-blue suit, leaned on the desk.

He noticed my hesitation and said, “Hey, young man, don’t be shy. Choose your lottery ticket.”

I picked up an envelope at random and turned it over. Number 13. Lucky me. The envelope wasn’t sealed and looked worn, as if it had been used many times before.

An attractive blonde behind the desk turned to look at me. “Don’t open the envelope until the buzzer sounds.” Her voice was stern, but her hazel eyes were wide and bright, like those of a deer caught in headlights.

“No problem,” I said. “Do you need to record which ticket I have?”

“No, you just write the ticket number and your full name at the top of the sheet. Is that number thirteen you drew?”

I turned the envelope to let her see it better.

“Uh-uh.” She nodded in commiseration. “Well, it might be your lucky number.”

“Oh, I’m sure it is,” I said lightly.

I flashed her my trademark crooked grin, feeling like an action hero from the spy flicks I loved watching as a boy. I even looked the part. Tall, athletic. Thick black hair with piercing blue eyes. My lips were not thin and cruel, though. I wasn’t a killer.

At least not yet.

A faint smile flickered at the corners of her lipstick-red mouth, and her eyes lingered on mine. I felt a tingle of electricity pass between us and mused how fine and silky her golden tresses would feel under my touch.

Highly inappropriate thoughts, given the circumstances. If I messed this up, there would be no easy escape for me—no cut, no second take.

She lifted her hand, letting me admire her slim wrist, and touched her hair reflexively, a wedding band glinting on her ring finger.

Married? Perfect.

In my line of work, I couldn’t afford to form emotional attachments, and I didn’t want to hurt those who liked me.

At that moment, a crowd of prospective students poured through the doorway, and the blonde looked away.

Was that a blush I saw just as she broke off eye contact?

Oh yes—it was, and with it the unmistakable flutter of her eyelashes.

Unnoticed by anyone else in the room, our private moment lasted only a few short seconds. The jovial bald man was talking to someone else, and not paying me the slightest attention.

Excellent.

I strolled into the hall and took a seat at a random table. Not so close to the mystery blonde that she’d think me too eager, but not too far, either.

Turning my head, ostensibly to look at the wall clock, I sneaked a peek at her out of the corner of my eye and saw her watching me.

I smiled to myself.

Perhaps I could glean the secrets of the Soviet education system from her and add them to my monthly report.

Unfortunately, the Russians had no tradition of wearing name tags, so I had no clue as to what her name might be, but I imagined it would be something glorious.

For some reason, I thought of Anna Karenina, no doubt because that was the novel I was reading at the time. Such a sad story.

Well, I was a modern man, not someone as thoughtless and destructive as Karenina’s toxic lover. If the mystery blonde and I were to have a secret story, it would be a happy tale.

Or so I hoped.

A loud buzzer rang, making me jump. In the silence that followed, the jovial bald-headed man announced that we were allowed to start. We would have three hours.

All around me, there was a rustle of papers being slid out of envelopes.

I opened mine, too.

As I expected, the questions and the assignment were pathetically easy. It took me slightly over an hour to complete them—much of that time spent on deciding whether to insert deliberate errors. But with over three hundred studious-looking young people competing for only twenty places, adding errors would be too much of a gamble.

After another hour of proofreading and daydreaming, I caught myself longing to escape this stuffy room and lose myself in a green park beneath shady trees, book in hand. But it wouldn’t be wise to bring attention to myself by being the first to leave, so I waited.

Now and again, I looked at the wall clock, each time taking a peek at the mystery blonde with my peripheral vision.

A couple of times, I caught her watching me, but she didn’t see me spying on her. After all, I was a professional trained by surveillance experts.

# Chapter 3: A Harmless Artist
In the final fifteen minutes before the exam deadline, the candidates began filing out of the room, handing their completed papers to the jolly old man (whom I privately began calling Bald Santa) and to the blonde beauty.

When the room was half-empty, I rose to my feet and gathered my papers. As I approached the entrance desk, she met my eyes briefly, favored me with a little smile, then looked away, studiously ignoring me.

I placed my papers on top of a stack in the center of the desk. After trading a few perfunctory pleasantries with Bald Santa, I stepped out of the room, but not before spotting her glancing at me sideways.

Outside, the university gardens were alive with sunshine and the hubbub of conversations. Hundreds of exam takers and students headed every which way or sat on benches. Young men smoked, girls gossiped.

I left the university grounds briefly to buy a bouquet of red and gold roses from a street stall and a large sheet of dense paper from a nearby art supply store.

After rolling the paper into a tube, I hid the flowers inside, stuffed wadded-up newspaper at both ends, then snapped on two rubber bands to keep it all together.

Carrying the bogus “art project” under my arm, I returned to the university gardens. I was an artist, after all. Well, that was my cover.

A hefty bribe had bought me a membership in the Artists Guild, which came with an eighty-ruble monthly grant, as well as the lease on an apartment that doubled as a painting studio in a town just outside Moscow.

Quickly deciding that the best way to blend into the crowd of students loitering outside the entrance was to light a cigarette, I pulled a midnight-blue pack of Cosmos out of my messenger bag—the best local brand, seventy kopeks a pack—and dove into the middle of the throng of young men hanging around neoclassical statues and unkempt rose bushes.

Someone offered me a light, and I passed the pack around.

Smokers are my favorite kind of people. So easy to befriend. Very soon, we began cracking jokes, laughing, and behaving like rowdy students all over the world.

Many of their jokes mocked the Communist Party in the most outrageous ways and made complimentary comments about the West and capitalism.

No one challenged the jokers. Communism was decidedly uncool.

I made a mental note to mention this in my report.

The group I joined consisted of freshmen and sophomores—not the applicants who had taken the exams. They all hoped my results would be good enough for me to be accepted into a program, so we could keep hanging out in the future.

As I chatted with them, I kept one eye on the colonnaded entrance. I had already determined that the numerous side and rear exits were used only by maintenance and security staff, so sooner or later the mystery blonde would have to pass my observation post.

The digital watch on my wrist showed 3:00 p.m.

The sun was sinking behind the soaring bulk of the main university building, its vast shadow creeping across the gardens—slow, deliberate, inevitable. High above, the spire rose into the pale sky, crowned by a golden star that blazed like a trapped flame.

The gardens darkened as the shadow passed, the air cooling and colors dimming. Perfect for anyone wishing to move unseen.

As most people worked nine to five, she would be out soon. Since it was Friday, many would leave early.

Unfortunately, my new friends soon departed.

But even as the milling crowd thinned, enough lingered to obscure me. I shifted position periodically, melting into gaps between students, letting the casual movement around me do the work of concealment.

Motion is commotion. And commotion is camouflage.
